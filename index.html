Passage (Set 11): In the middle of the night, Shankar was waken up by a strange loud noise from the forest. There seemed to be a huge turmoil in the jungles. Alvarez too had been up. They listen carefully - and what they heard was truly strange. They had no clue about what was happening. Shankar switched on his torch and was about to leave the tent, but Alvarez stopped him. He said, "Don't rush out in these unknown jungles at night. I have cautioned you many times. Besides, where is your riffle?" It was pitched dark outside. What they saw in the torch light defied logic. Wild animals were running desperately, trampling on bushes and small plants. They came out of the mountain forest on the west and ran towards the hill on the east. There were hyenas, baboons and wild buffalos. Two leopards passed close by them.

Passage (Set 13): The tomb of Humayun, the second Mughal emperor was built between 1569 and 1571 by the Persian architect Mirak Mirza Ghiyath under the instructions of Humayun's grieving widow, Hamida Banu Begum. The oldest garden-tomb in the Indian sub-continent, it is the first example of the Persian influence on Indian architecture. It houses more than 1000 graves, which has led to it being called 'the Dormitory of the Mughals'. It houses the tombs of the Mughal emperors, Jahandar Shah and Farrukh Siyar, and also of Darah Shikoh - Shahjahan's murdered son. The tomb is a protected monument which means the defacing it in any way would cost you Rs. 5000 or three months in prison. It is on the world Heritage list, and recognized internationally as a monument that deserves protection for the benefit of all humanity. A massive restoration process was carried out over four years to ensure that the maintenance of the tomb met international standards. Between the years 2000 and 2003, the Agha Khan Trust for Culture, in collaboration with the Archaeological Survey of India, carried out a project to revitalise the 30 acre garden and the monument itself. The tomb is located near the shrine of an important Sufi Saint, Nizamuddin Auliya. It is considered auspicious to be buried near the tomb of a saint, and there are various other monuments outside the main tomb.

Passage (Set 14): Man alone of all creatures thinks of things as tools and uses them as such. Through his inventive genius a stick became a club with which he defended himself from the wild beast and killed the poisonous serpent. Soon he saw that if it was sharp he could thrust with it and he had spear. Then he threw its point first, and had a javelin. Simple as this seems to us now, each step in advance was a tremendous one for a primitive man, and when, perhaps ages later, some man found that he could bend another stick with a cord stretched from end to end and shoot the javelin from that, thus having a bow and arrow, how great a further advance it was! It is interesting to see how man took the things which he found at hand and turned to his own uses. Primitive man could cross shallow rivers by wading, but some streams were too deep, too swift or too wide for wading or swimming. Across a swift narrow one he might find a fallen tree and thus get the idea of a bridge. Across the wide, placid ones he could float a fallen log and find his first boat supplied by nature. All bridges and all ships have grown from these simple beginnings. In the same way, the stone which was his first hammer, was his first axe when it had a sharp edge. When it was both sharp and slim, it became his first knife. From these two have come all the numberless kinds of cutting tools.

Passage (Set 15): The Hindu temple is the house of deity. We go to see them and be seen by them, no different from visiting a relative's or friend's house, or going to a king's court, with a petition. The practice of invoking and adoring a deity and then petitioning him for material benefits informed the ancient Vedic ritual known as yagna. It continued to inform the late temple rituals known as puja. What makes puja different from Yagna? however, it is the value placed on darshan. The word 'darshan' has a double meaning : view as well as world view, sight as well as insight. It is simultaneously about doing and thinking, action and introspection. Hindu deities have elaborate from, their images charged with hymn and ritual, are capable of responding to the prayers of the devotee. Darshan acknowledges the value of relationship, between deity and devotee, between self and other.
